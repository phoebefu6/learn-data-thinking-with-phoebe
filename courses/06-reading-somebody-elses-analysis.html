Reading somebody else's analysis

Almost every number you act on was produced by somebody else, in a hurry, for a slightly different question than the one you have. You will not get to see their rows, you will usually not get to see their method, and you have about ninety seconds in a meeting. Six questions do most of the work, they are all askable without sounding like an interrogation, and the order matters because the early ones find the biggest problems. Then a final scorecard on a real analysis, and where to go next.

Part 1 · covers the six questions

Six questions, in the order that finds things fastest

7 min live

Ordered by how much they typically find per second spent. The first three are almost always worth asking; the last three are for when the decision is expensive.

LiveThe first three, and what each one catches4 min▶

Compared with what? A conversion rate of 4 percent is neither good nor bad until you know what it is being held against: last quarter, the other channel, the target, the industry. Numbers presented without a comparison are asking you to supply one from memory, and you will supply a flattering one.

Who is missing from this? Charts are made from rows that survived. Customers who churned before the survey, orders that errored out, the branch whose data feed broke in March. The rows that dropped out never appear, and their absence looks exactly like them not existing. This is the question that catches the analysis where a whole group silently left.

Did the mix change? Four words, ten seconds, and the single highest-yield question in the set. If the composition of the population shifted between the two periods being compared, the headline is a blend of two things moving at once, and it can point the opposite way to every one of its parts. Session 4 showed exactly that: a headline up thirteen pence while email orders fell by GBP 3.83 and social orders by GBP 2.97.

Self-studyHow to ask without becoming the difficult person3 min▶

All six questions can be asked in a way that sounds like interest or like an accusation, and the difference is almost entirely whether you attach a reason. "Did the mix change?" alone sounds like a challenge. "Did the mix change - I ask because we shifted spend to social in that window and it would pull the average up on its own" sounds like somebody helping.

Two more habits that keep it collegial. Ask about the work rather than the person: "what does this compare against" rather than "why didn't you compare it". And ask your own numbers the same questions in public, visibly, so that the six questions read as house style rather than as something that happens to other people's work.

The one exception is when the decision is large and irreversible. Then the questions get asked properly, in writing, and the politeness budget goes on tone rather than on skipping any of them.

The story around a number travels further than the number

LiveWhy the second one is the more useful lesson3 min▶

The Berkeley numbers are correct and the conclusion drawn from them is correct. What is wrong is the narrative wrapped around them, and the narrative is the part everybody remembers and repeats. That is worth sitting with: the single most-cited example of a misleading aggregate is itself surrounded by a claim that nobody checked, in textbooks, for decades.

It is also worth noticing what the careful version of the finding actually was. The authors did not conclude "no bias". They found four departments significantly biased against women and six significantly biased against men, and their pooled figure showed a small but statistically significant bias in favour of women. Nearly every retelling flattens that into something cleaner than what the paper said, in the same way that the 80 percent claim flattens two survey answers into one number.

The pattern is the same in both cases and it is the thing to watch for: the tidy version travels and the careful version does not. When you hear a number with a good story attached, the story is the part most likely to be wrong.

Self-studyThe tiers these sessions used on themselves3 min▶

Every factual claim across these six sessions carries a tier. Read directly from the primary source, read from a reliable secondary that quotes the primary, or seen only in a search result. The Berkeley figures above are the middle tier: the 1975 paper itself would not load on any attempt during this build, so the numbers come from three independent sources that reproduce its tables and agree with each other to the digit. That is good enough to teach from and not good enough to call first-hand, and the difference is stated rather than smoothed over.

You can do the same thing in your own work at almost no cost. When you quote a figure, write next to it where you got it and whether you saw the original. Three months later, when somebody challenges it, that one line is the difference between defending your number and discovering you cannot.

Build-along

Read one, then score the course

★ 22 min · a real analysis

The chart or slide you brought. Twelve minutes reading it properly, then ten on the scorecard.

State the claim in one sentence, in your own words, as an outcome and a direction. If you cannot, that is already the finding: the analysis has not told you what it thinks is true.

Ask the first three of the chart rather than the person: what is this compared with, who could have dropped out before this was drawn, and did the composition change between the periods shown. Write what you can answer from the page alone.

Ask the last three. What is the denominator and is it the right one; how many links back is the source; and what result would have changed the author's conclusion.

Write the one question you will actually ask, with its reason attached. One is the right number: it gets answered, whereas six at once gets defended.

Score the card below honestly, including the rows you know you skipped.

If you take one thing from the whole course. Most wrong answers are answers to the wrong question, and most of the rest are answers nobody checked twice. Both failures are cheap to prevent and expensive to discover later, and neither needs any mathematics.

Homework

After the course

25 min
  1. Ask your one question and note what comes back. The answer is often "I do not know", which is not a failure of the analysis; it is the beginning of the next one.
  2. Put the six questions somewhere you will see them before your next review meeting. They take ninety seconds and they are the whole of the six sessions in a form you can use while somebody is talking.
  3. Trace one more number to its source. Every organisation has three or four figures everybody quotes and nobody can place. Finding the origin of one is a genuinely useful afternoon.
  4. Pick the next course by what is actually blocking you: whether a difference is real, how to design the comparison, whether the chart is honest, or whether the question finally needs a model.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · You have ninety seconds with a chart in a meeting. Which three questions do you ask first?

The first three are answerable from the page itself and catch the largest problems: no comparison, a group that silently dropped out, and a composition shift that makes the headline a blend of two moving things. Significance and intervals matter, and they are a later rung and a different course.

2 · The Berkeley admissions case: the figures and the reversal are real, but the widely told lawsuit story appears to be false. What is the lesson?

The numbers survive scrutiny; the narrative did not. The same flattening happened to the paper's actual conclusion, which was more careful than the retellings, and to the 80 percent claim, where two survey answers became one number. The pattern repeats because tidy stories are easier to carry.

3 · You have six good questions about a colleague's analysis. What do you actually do?

Attaching the reason is what turns a challenge into help: "did the mix change, I ask because we moved spend to social that month" invites an answer. Volume triggers defence. The exception is genuinely expensive decisions, where the politeness budget goes on tone rather than on skipping questions.
