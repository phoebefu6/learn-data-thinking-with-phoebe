Learn Data Thinking with Phoebe

A promotion ran, and the headline said revenue per order was flat. Up thirteen pence. Nothing to see. Split it by channel and every single channel had fallen by three to four pounds, because the promotion pulled in orders through the more expensive channel and the mix did the rest. The headline was arithmetically correct and completely wrong. Six sessions on the work that happens before anybody builds a model: turning a vague request into a question that has an answer, working out what you would need to know, looking at the data before calculating anything, and recognising the three or four ways a simple number misleads. No maths beyond averages, no code required.

The six sessions 🎯 · before any model exists

The running situation is Daybreak, the coffee-subscription company from the sibling courses. They ran an autumn promotion, and three people want to know whether it worked. They want three different things, only two of those questions can be answered with the data that exists, and the answer to one of them reverses the moment you look properly.

❓ Session 1 · easy

The question behind the question

"Can you look at the numbers" is not a question. What turns a request into something answerable: an outcome, a population, a timeframe, and the decision waiting on the other side of it.

▶ Start here 🧾 Session 2 · easy

What you would need to know

Work out the ideal data before you look at the data you have, because doing it the other way round quietly rewrites the question into whatever the spreadsheet can answer.

the gap list 🔍 Session 3 · easy

Look before you model

The first twenty minutes with any dataset: how many rows, what is missing, what is duplicated, what is impossible. The problems that break every later step are visible here and nowhere else.

first twenty minutes 📊 Session 4 · bench night

The four answers bench

One dataset, three questions, four levels of answer each. One headline survives every level, one reverses completely, and one question cannot be answered at all. You cannot tell which is which by looking at the headline.

★ the bench ➗ Session 5 · medium

When a count is the answer

Most questions worth asking are answered by a count, a rate or a comparison. Knowing when to stop is a skill, and reaching for a model when a count would do is the commonest expensive mistake in the job.

knowing when to stop 🧐 Session 6 · medium

Reading somebody else's analysis

The questions that find the weak spot in any chart or deck put in front of you, in the order worth asking them. Final scorecard, and where to go next on the shelf.

What is honest here 🧾

The bench in session 4 computes every number from its own rows. The dataset behind it is constructed, and the pages say so, because the real-world anchor for what it demonstrates is a published case rather than this one.
